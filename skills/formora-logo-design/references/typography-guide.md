# Typography Guide: Letters as Design Material

Load this file whenever a concept uses letters, a wordmark, an Arabic name, or a bilingual lockup. Treat every letter as a set of parts that can carry a second meaning, but never let a part lose the job it does for reading.

## 1. Latin anatomy as fusion material

Name the part you are transforming. "Turn the R into a falcon" is vague; "the R's leg becomes the falcon's tail, the bowl becomes the head" is a construction.

| Part | What it is | What it tends to become |
|---|---|---|
| Stem | Main vertical (or near-vertical) stroke | Trunk, tower, spine, pillar, leg, tool handle, beak line |
| Bowl | Curved stroke enclosing a counter (b, d, p, B, R) | Head, body, belly, fruit, bean, wheel, lens |
| Counter | Enclosed or partly enclosed space inside a letter | Eye, window, doorway, keyhole, drop; the main place to hide a second subject |
| Crossbar | Horizontal stroke joining or crossing stems (A, H, e, t) | Horizon, wing span, bridge, shelf, road, mouth |
| Terminal | End of a stroke that has no serif | Head, tail tip, beak, leaf tip, flame, claw |
| Aperture | Opening of a partly enclosed counter (c, e, s, a) | Mouth, entrance, open hand, wave; widening it adds openness, closing it adds tension |
| Diagonal | Angled stroke (A, K, M, N, V, W, X, Y) | Wing, ear, horn, roofline, mountain, arrow, motion |

Other parts worth naming: arm (E, F, upper K), leg (R, K), tail (Q, y), spine (S), apex and vertex (points of A, V, W), ascender and descender, shoulder (n, h, m).

Rules:
- Transform one or two parts. Keep the rest conventional so the letter still reads.
- Counters are the cheapest place for a hidden subject because changing them barely alters the outer silhouette.
- Terminals and diagonals give a creature its direction and character; decide which way it faces before drawing.
- Keep stroke weight consistent between the "letter" parts and the "image" parts, or the fusion reads as two drawings glued together.

## 2. Distortion limits and recognition features

A letter stops reading when its distinguishing feature is lost, not when it is changed a lot. Identify that feature first and protect it.

| Letter | Feature that carries recognition | Safe to change |
|---|---|---|
| A | Apex with two diagonals | Crossbar can drop, tilt or become a shape if context keeps it an A |
| B | Two stacked bowls on one stem | Bowl sizes, stem length, the join between bowls |
| C | Single curve with a clear open aperture | Terminals, weight contrast |
| E | Stem with three arms | Middle arm length, arm endings |
| G | C-shape plus spur or bar | Everything except the bar; without it G becomes C |
| H | Two stems linked by a crossbar | Crossbar height, stem endings |
| K | Stem with two diagonals meeting at or near it | Diagonal angles and endings |
| M / W | Count and direction of the points | Point depth; flipping one into the other changes the letter |
| O | Closed, unbroken round counter | Proportion, inner shape; a break can turn it into C or G |
| R | Bowl plus a leg | Leg shape; without the leg R becomes P |
| S | Reversing spine with two opposite apertures | Weight, terminals; the reverse curve must survive |
| V / Y | Single vertex; Y adds a stem | Arm angles |
| e | Horizontal bar and lower aperture | Bar angle, aperture width |

Tests:
- Context lowers the bar. A distorted letter inside a word reads more easily than the same letter alone as a monogram. A single-letter mark needs the highest fidelity.
- Show the mark for two seconds to someone who does not know the name. If they cannot say the letter, the distortion went too far, unless illegibility was a deliberate choice the user approved.
- Check at 16 px and in solid black. Fine distinguishing details (a thin crossbar, a small spur) disappear first.
- Never rely on colour to tell a letter part from an image part.

## 3. Wordmarks

Custom letterforms
- Start from a real typeface skeleton or a clear construction grid, then customise two or three letters that carry the concept. Redrawing every letter rarely improves a name and costs consistency.
- Repeat shared decisions across the word: same terminal cut, same corner radius, same stroke contrast, same counter shape.
- Watch letter pairs that create accidental words or shapes (rn reading as m, cl as d, unintended symbols in negative space).

Spacing
- Space optically, not by numbers. Equalise the area of the gaps, not the distance between stems. Round letters (O, C) sit closer to neighbours than straight ones.
- All-caps settings usually need more tracking than lowercase. Large display sizes need tighter tracking than small ones.
- Kern every pair by hand in the final artwork; default kerning is not finished work.

Weight
- Match the wordmark's stroke weight to the symbol's dominant stroke, or make a clear, deliberate contrast. Near-matches look like mistakes.
- Very thin weights fail at small sizes and in embroidery, engraving and low-resolution screens. Test the lightest weight at the minimum size.

Case
- All caps: stable, formal, monumental; harder to read in long names.
- Lowercase: approachable, contemporary; ascenders and descenders give the word a distinctive outline.
- Title case: neutral and familiar. Choose case for the brand's personality, not by habit.

Pairing a symbol with a wordmark
- Derive both from the same construction (shared geometry, angles, terminals). The symbol should look like it was drawn with the same tool as the letters.
- Set the symbol's height against a typographic line (cap height, x-height, or full ascender-to-descender height) and adjust optically.
- Design at least a horizontal and a stacked lockup. Define clear space using a unit from the mark itself (for example the counter of a letter).
- The symbol must also stand alone. If it only makes sense next to the name, it is not finished.

## 4. Arabic

Direction and joining
- Arabic is written and read right to left. The word starts on the right; a horizontal lockup's visual flow runs leftward. Decide which way any creature or object faces with that flow in mind, and never mirror a Latin layout mechanically.
- Arabic has no upper or lower case. There is no capital to build a monogram on.
- Most letters join to the letters on both sides. Six letters never join to the following letter: ا د ذ ر ز و. Each one breaks the word, so a word is a series of connected groups, not one stroke. Hamza (ء) stands alone. Lam followed by alif must form the lam-alif ligature (لا).
- A concept that needs one continuous stroke across the whole word fails if the name contains a non-joining letter. Check the spelling before proposing it.

Contextual forms
- A joining letter has four forms: isolated, initial (joins left only), medial (joins both sides), final (joins right only). Non-joining letters have only isolated and final forms.
- The forms can look very different. The initial form of a letter is often a small tooth or partial shape, so the first letter of an Arabic name is a weak base for a monogram compared with a Latin capital.
- Always design with the form the letter actually takes in this name. Do not take an isolated letter from a chart and drop it into a word.

Dots (i'jam)
- Many letters share one skeleton and are told apart only by the number and position of dots: ب ت ث (and the initial and medial forms of ن ي), ج ح خ, د ذ, ر ز, س ش, ص ض, ط ظ, ع غ, ف ق.
- Dots carry identity. Moving, merging, dropping or adding a dot changes the letter and can change or destroy the word. Count them in every draft.
- Dots are also fusion material: an eye, a seed, a drop, a star, a coin. The transformed dot must stay in its correct position, count and rough size relative to the letter.
- Dot shape follows the style: rhombic in pen-based styles such as Naskh and Thuluth, often square or circular in geometric Kufi. Keep one dot shape across the mark.
- Short-vowel marks (harakat) are normally omitted in logos. If the name is ambiguous without them, ask the user how it should be read.

Baseline, verticals and kashida
- Letters sit on a horizontal baseline; joins run along it. Several letters descend below it, especially in final form (for example ر ز و and the final forms of ن ي ع م). Tall verticals such as alif (ا) and lam (ل) rise above.
- Verticals are natural material for posts, stems, necks, minarets, masts. Descending curves suit tails, waves, hulls and wings.
- Kashida (tatweel) is the elongation of the connecting stroke between two joined letters. It can stretch a word to a width or give a bird its wing or a road its length.
- Kashida only goes between letters that join. Never place it after a non-joining letter, which cannot connect forward. One deliberate extension reads as craft; several read as filler. Keep the extended stroke in the same weight and logic as the script.

Script styles

| Style | Character | Logo suitability |
|---|---|---|
| Kufi | Angular, geometric, strong horizontals and verticals; includes square (grid) Kufi | Best suited to geometric construction, grids, monoline marks and negative-space work. Heavily abstracted square Kufi can stop reading; test it |
| Naskh | The standard text script; balanced and highly readable | Best base when the name must read instantly; good for wordmarks and bilingual pairing |
| Thuluth | Monumental, large curves, tall verticals, stacked and overlapping letters | Strong for prestige and heritage, but complex; simplify heavily and test small sizes and readability |
| Diwani | Highly cursive and ornate, letters tightly interlocked | Elegant for luxury and ceremony; often hard for general readers and weak at small sizes |
| Ruq'ah | Everyday handwriting; compact, simple, sits close to the baseline, dots often joined into dashes | Good for informal, quick, friendly brands; efficient and readable |

Use a style the way a calligrapher would. Mixing letter shapes from different styles in one word looks wrong to native readers even when they cannot say why.

Why a Latin construction cannot be transposed
- Latin letters are separate units with fixed shapes; Arabic letters change shape by position and join into groups.
- Latin marks lean on capitals, cap height and vertical stems; Arabic weight lives on the baseline, in horizontal joins, dots and descending curves.
- The proportions, rhythm and density differ, and direction is reversed. A W-wolf idea has no direct Arabic equivalent; find the animal or object in the Arabic word's own shapes instead.

Readability test (mandatory before approval)
- Show the mark to a native Arabic reader without telling them the name. They must read it correctly on their own.
- If they hesitate, misread a letter, or read a different word, revise. Check dots first, then joins, then the forms used.
- Note who did the test. If no native reader is available, tell the user the Arabic has not been validated.

## 5. Bilingual lockups

- Design the Arabic and Latin versions as siblings, not mirror copies. Each is drawn natively in its own script, with the same concept, weight and proportion.
- Match apparent weight and size, not measurements. Arabic has no cap height and different internal proportions, so the same point size often looks smaller or lighter. Adjust by eye until both carry equal visual weight.
- Share the construction language: stroke contrast, terminal treatment, corner radius, dot or counter shape, angle system.
- Express the concept in each script's own material. If the Latin mark hides an eye in the counter of an O, the Arabic version might use a dot or a counter of a suitable Arabic letter, not a copy of the O.
- Choose the alignment line on purpose (shared baseline, optical centre, or top and bottom of the symbol) and check it by eye.
- Decide which script leads from the primary market. In a horizontal lockup the Arabic side reads from the right; keep each script's direction intact.
- Test both versions separately and together, in monochrome and at minimum size.

## 6. Text in generated images

- Image generators frequently misspell words, add or drop letters, and invent letter-like shapes.
- They usually break Arabic: letters left unjoined, joins in the wrong place, wrong contextual forms, missing or extra dots, left-to-right order, or decorative pseudo-script that is not Arabic.
- Check every piece of text in every generated image letter by letter, and for Arabic check joins, forms and dots against the correct spelling.
- Treat generated lettering as a sketch of the idea only. Build final lettering as vector paths, drawn or adapted from a correctly licensed typeface, and re-check spelling in the vector file.
- Never tell the user a generated image's text is correct without having checked it.

## 7. Recommending typefaces

- Describe type by classification and character first: for example "geometric sans, monoline, round counters, low contrast" or "high-contrast Naskh-based display, compact, soft terminals".
- Name a specific typeface only when confident it exists, it supports the needed script (check Arabic support separately), and the description matches it. If unsure, give the description alone.
- Always tell the user to check the typeface's licence for logo and commercial use before adopting it. Some licences restrict logos, modification or embedding.
- If the wordmark is customised from a typeface, say which letters were modified and that the licence must allow modification.
