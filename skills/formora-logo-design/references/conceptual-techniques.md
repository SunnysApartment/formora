# Conceptual Techniques

Seven fusion techniques. A concept may combine several. For every technique, run the "find the fusion" method on paper (stroke list, feature list, shared geometry) before generating any image.

General method for all techniques:
1. List the letter's parts: stems, bowls, arms, diagonals, counters (enclosed holes), terminals, joins, apex/vertex points.
2. List the subject's defining features: the 3-5 parts that make it recognizable in silhouette (ear, horn, beak, wing, tail, eye), plus its overall outline.
3. Look for shared geometry: a letter part and a subject part with the same direction, curve, angle or position. Build the mark on that overlap.
4. If no overlap exists, change the letter, the subject, the subject's pose, or the technique. Never force it with decoration.

## 1. Letterform Morphing

**What it is:** One or more letters are reshaped into a recognizable form while remaining the letter.

**Suits a brief when:** the brand has a strong initial or short name, and the subject has a body or outline close to the letter's skeleton (S and snake, W and wolf anatomy).

**Examples:**
- `W` becomes the outline or anatomy of a wolf.
- `S` becomes a snake.
- `E` becomes a structured architectural element.
- A letter's counter, terminal or stroke becomes an object component.

**Design test:** The original letter must remain identifiable where the concept depends on reading it.

**Find the fusion:** Write the letter's stroke path as one continuous line if possible. Overlay the subject's spine or key contour on it. Adjust only the strokes that must change; keep the letter's stem count, counter count and baseline/cap-height relationship.

**Typical failure:** The subject wins and the letter disappears, or a letter is drawn with an animal pasted onto it. Check by blurring: the letter must still read.

## 2. Silhouette Integration

**What it is:** The wordmark or selected letters form part of an animal or object silhouette.

**Suits a brief when:** a full name or several letters are available, the subject has a distinctive outline, and a bold, compact mark is wanted.

**Examples:**
- A word arranged to create the profile of a bird.
- A brand initial embedded into the outline of a fish.
- Several letters forming the body and limbs of an animal.

**Design test:** The silhouette stays coherent and the typography does not become arbitrary decoration.

**Find the fusion:** Draw the subject's silhouette first, simplified to 6-10 anchor points. Assign each letter or letter part to a body region it already resembles (arch to back, diagonal to leg, bowl to head or haunch). Keep the letters' order along the reading path.

**Typical failure:** Letters are scaled or rotated until unreadable, or the outline is only suggested so the animal is not recognized. Test the silhouette filled solid.

## 3. Negative-Space Fusion

**What it is:** The empty space between or inside shapes reveals a second subject.

**Suits a brief when:** a second meaning is wanted as a reward on closer look, and the primary form can be a strong solid shape or letter.

**Examples:**
- Two opposing shapes reveal an animal head.
- Letter spacing creates a hidden object.
- A symbol contains a secondary letter through its counter-space.

**Design test:** The hidden subject is discoverable without making the primary symbol confusing.

**Find the fusion:** Decide which subject is figure and which is ground. Trace the ground subject's outline, then check that its edges are made by the figure's edges (the figure must be shaped by the ground subject, not the reverse by accident). Adjust gaps, counters and letter spacing until the ground subject has a closed, recognizable contour.

**Typical failure:** The hidden form needs color or a prior hint to be seen, or its edges are broken by uneven gaps. Test in black on white and at small size.

## 4. Shape Fusion

**What it is:** Two visual subjects merge into one shape.

**Suits a brief when:** the brief supplies two non-letter subjects (an animal and an industry object) or a letter plus an object, and a single continuous contour is wanted.

**Examples:**
- An animal and an industry-related object become one symbol.
- A leaf and a letterform share the same contour.
- A tool and an animal are one continuous silhouette.

**Design test:** The two elements must share a plausible visual structure, not simply overlap.

**Find the fusion:** Simplify both subjects to primitives (circle, arc, wedge, line). Find the shared primitive (a leaf's curved edge and a wing's leading edge; a crown's points and horns). Build one contour that serves both readings, then remove any line that serves only one.

**Typical failure:** Overlap or stacking presented as fusion; or a contour that reads as one subject plus noise. Each part of the outline must pull double duty.

## 5. Typography Illustration

**What it is:** An illustration constructed from letters, words or typographic strokes.

**Suits a brief when:** the name is the hero, a long name resists a compact symbol, or the brand wants a lettering-led, expressive mark.

**Examples:**
- A word forms the outline of an animal.
- Repeated letterforms create a recognizable object.
- The brand name becomes the main illustration rather than a separate caption.

**Design test:** The result remains legible enough for its intended application.

**Find the fusion:** Decide the legibility budget first (full word readable, or initial only). Set the letters in a fixed weight and skeleton, then deform them by one consistent rule (stretch, curve, extend one terminal). Let the rule, not decoration, create the subject.

**Typical failure:** Illustration density destroys letter recognition; inconsistent stroke weight; effects added to rescue weak construction.

## 6. Symbolic Geometry

**What it is:** Circles, arcs, lines, grids and primitives merge multiple meanings into one construction.

**Suits a brief when:** the brand is technical, precise or architectural, a minimal mark is wanted, or the mark must be built reproducibly as vector.

**Examples:**
- A geometric initial becomes an animal profile.
- A circular construction represents both an object and a letter.
- Repeated geometry creates a unified emblem.

**Design test:** Geometry must improve proportion and coherence, not impose unnecessary complexity.

**Find the fusion:** Choose a small kit (one circle size, one angle, one stroke width). Draw the letter using only the kit, then the subject using only the kit, and compare. Where both can be drawn from the same circles or angles, the fusion exists. Fewer primitives is better.

**Typical failure:** Construction lines visible but meaningless, geometry so strict the subject is unrecognizable, or a grid pattern that could fit any brand.

## 7. Hidden Letterforms

**What it is:** Brand initials are integrated into an illustration so the letter is part of the subject's construction.

**Suits a brief when:** an illustrated subject is required (animal, object, emblem) and the initial must be carried without a separate lettering element.

**Examples:**
- Initials hidden in an animal's body.
- A letter created by the negative space between two objects.
- A monogram that also reads as an industry-related symbol.

**Design test:** The letter should feel structurally necessary to the mark.

**Find the fusion:** Place the letter's skeleton over the subject and find where its strokes coincide with existing anatomy (a limb as a stem, a horn pair as a V). Strengthen those coincidences and weaken the rest. Remove the letter in your head: if the mark is unchanged, the letter is not structural.

**Typical failure:** The letter is only visible with an explanation, or is a separate overlay. Aim for a letter that is found in one or two seconds, not never.

## Finding five different directions

Five directions must differ in mechanism, not in surface. Two concepts that use the same mechanism with a different animal, pose or letter are one concept.

Method:
1. Name the mechanism of each idea in a few words (for example "letter becomes subject", "subject hidden in gap", "name is the illustration"). Mechanisms map to the seven techniques above.
2. Assign each direction a different primary technique. Use at most one direction per technique unless the brief is narrow.
3. Vary which element leads: letter-led, subject-led, name-led, space-led, geometry-led.
4. Vary the letter or part used (initial, counter of a middle letter, the full name) where the brief allows.
5. Cross-check pairwise: if two directions can be described by the same sentence with one noun swapped, replace one.
6. For each direction record the fields in `templates/concept-brief.md`: concept name, primary element, secondary element, typography opportunity, relationship, symbolic meaning, construction method, brand fit, risks.

Illustration, brief WOLF (outdoor equipment; strength, exploration, resilience):

| Direction | Mechanism | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| 01 Letter Animal: the `W` becomes a wolf silhouette | Letter becomes subject | Letterform Morphing |
| 02 Hidden Symbol: wolf eyes integrated into the `O` | Subject detail lives in a letter part | Hidden Letterforms |
| 03 Typography: wordmark strokes reference wolf anatomy | Name is the illustration | Typography Illustration |
| 04 Negative Space: wolf profile appears between letterforms | Subject lives in the gaps | Negative-Space Fusion |
| 05 Geometric Fusion: `W` and wolf head share one geometric structure | One construction kit serves both | Symbolic Geometry |

This table illustrates the method; it is not a prescribed design for any brand. If the first five do not suit, produce more directions with new mechanisms rather than variants.

## Worked example briefs

**A. Coffee: coffee + eagle + letter E.** Directions: E becomes the eagle's wing; eagle head in the E's negative space; wordmark forms an eagle silhouette; coffee bean and eagle profile share one outline; eagle wing becomes the E's central stroke.

**B. Fitness: strength + bull + letter B.** Directions: B's bowls form the bull's head structure; horns extend from the upper letterform; negative space reveals a bull profile; geometric bull silhouette built from the B; wordmark with muscular custom-built letterforms.

**C. Technology: AI + owl + letter A.** Directions: A's counter becomes the owl's eyes; owl silhouette built from angular letter strokes; owl wings form the A's crossbar; circuit-like structure forms part of the owl's face; initial and owl share one geometric outline.

**D. Real estate: architecture + falcon + letter R.** Directions: R's leg becomes a building structure; falcon profile through negative space; bird wing becomes an architectural roofline; initial and building silhouette share one contour; wordmark with architectural proportions.

**E. Arabic brand: Arabic name + falcon.** Directions: Arabic letter strokes form the bird silhouette; the falcon's wing follows the movement of the script; a letter counter reveals the eye; the bird profile emerges from connected letterforms; a standalone symbol derived from the same construction. Account for script direction, letter joining, contextual forms and readability. Do not transfer a Latin construction directly; build from the Arabic letters' own skeletons.
