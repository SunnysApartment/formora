## 1. Overview

FORMORA is a conceptual logo-design methodology packaged as a reusable
Claude Code skill.

Its purpose is to help create logos in which two or more visual ideas
are intelligently combined into one unified mark. The skill treats
typography, letters, words, animals, objects, silhouettes, geometry, and
negative space as materials that can be transformed and fused.

FORMORA is **not** intended to be a generic logo generator that places
an icon next to a wordmark. It should behave like a conceptual identity
designer: understand the brand, discover an original visual
relationship, explain the concept, construct the mark, and refine it for
practical use.

### The central principle

**One concept → Two visual elements → One unified symbol.**

Examples of the intended outcomes:

-   A brand name whose letters form an animal silhouette.
-   An animal whose anatomy incorporates the brand's initials.
-   A letter transformed into an object associated with the business.
-   Two objects or concepts merged into a single recognizable symbol.
-   Negative space that reveals a hidden letter, animal, or secondary
    meaning.
-   A wordmark whose letterforms communicate the brand's core idea.

### Name rationale

**FORMORA** combines the ideas of *form*, *morphing*, and *aura*:
transforming visual forms into meaningful identity systems.

## 2. Design Philosophy

A strong conceptual logo should reward recognition. At first glance, the
viewer should see a clear, coherent mark. On closer inspection, they
should discover the second meaning or the clever construction behind it.

FORMORA should prioritize:

1.  **Concept before decoration.** Every visual decision must support
    the idea.
2.  **Integration over juxtaposition.** Elements should become one
    composition, not sit beside each other.
3.  **Clarity over complexity.** The symbol should remain understandable
    at a glance.
4.  **Distinctiveness over trends.** Avoid generic logo aesthetics and
    fashionable effects that do not serve the concept.
5.  **Typography as material.** Letters can be reshaped, extended,
    joined, or used as structural components.
6.  **Meaningful negative space.** Empty space can carry as much meaning
    as the positive shapes.
7.  **Practicality.** The mark must work in monochrome, at small sizes,
    and across real brand applications.

## 4. Core Design Techniques

FORMORA should explore the following techniques. A single concept may
combine multiple techniques when doing so improves the design.

### 4.1 Letterform Morphing

Transform one or more letters into recognizable visual forms.

Examples:

-   The letter `W` becomes the outline or anatomy of a wolf.
-   The letter `S` becomes a snake.
-   The letter `E` becomes a structured architectural element.
-   A letter's counter, terminal, or stroke becomes a recognizable
    object component.

**Design test:** The original letter should remain identifiable where
the concept depends on reading it.

### 4.2 Silhouette Integration

Construct the wordmark or selected letterforms so that they form part of
an animal or object silhouette.

Examples:

-   A word arranged to create the profile of a bird.
-   A brand initial embedded into the outline of a fish.
-   Several letters forming the body and limbs of an animal.

**Design test:** The silhouette should remain coherent and the
typography should not become arbitrary decoration.

### 4.3 Negative-Space Fusion

Use the empty space between or inside shapes to reveal a second subject.

Examples:

-   Two opposing shapes reveal an animal head.
-   Letter spacing creates a hidden object.
-   A symbol contains a secondary letter through its counter-space.

**Design test:** The hidden subject should be discoverable without
making the primary symbol confusing.

### 4.4 Shape Fusion

Merge two visual subjects into a single shape.

Examples:

-   An animal and an industry-related object become one symbol.
-   A leaf and a letterform share the same contour.
-   A tool and an animal are represented by a single continuous
    silhouette.

**Design test:** The two elements must share a plausible visual
structure rather than simply overlap.

### 4.5 Typography Illustration

Construct an illustration using letters, words, or typographic strokes.

Examples:

-   A word forms the outline of an animal.
-   Repeated letterforms create a recognizable object.
-   The brand name becomes the main illustration rather than a separate
    caption.

**Design test:** The result must remain legible enough for its intended
application.

### 4.6 Symbolic Geometry

Use circles, arcs, lines, grids, and geometric primitives to merge
multiple meanings.

Examples:

-   A geometric initial becomes an animal profile.
-   A circular construction represents both an object and a letter.
-   Repeated geometry creates a unified emblem.

**Design test:** Geometry must improve proportion and coherence, not
impose unnecessary complexity.

### 4.7 Hidden Letterforms

Integrate brand initials into an illustration so that the letter is part
of the subject's construction.

Examples:

-   Initials hidden in an animal's body.
-   A letter created by the negative space between two objects.
-   A monogram that also reads as an industry-related symbol.

**Design test:** The letter should feel structurally necessary to the
mark.

## 5. End-to-End Workflow

FORMORA should follow a deliberate, repeatable process.

### Phase 1 --- Brand Intelligence

Collect or infer, where appropriate:

-   Brand name and spelling.
-   Industry and business category.
-   Products or services.
-   Target audience.
-   Brand personality and values.
-   Desired positioning.
-   Cultural or language considerations.
-   Required applications and constraints.
-   Existing brand assets or references.

Do not invent brand facts. Ask concise questions when essential
information is missing.

### Phase 2 --- Concept Discovery

Develop at least five genuinely different conceptual directions.

For each direction, document:

-   Concept name.
-   Primary visual element.
-   Secondary visual element.
-   Typography opportunity.
-   Visual relationship between the elements.
-   Symbolic meaning.
-   Proposed construction method.
-   Why the concept fits the brand.
-   Potential risks or legibility concerns.

Do not create five superficial variations of the same idea. Explore
different conceptual mechanisms.

### Phase 3 --- Visual Fusion

Explore suitable combinations of:

-   Letterform morphing.
-   Silhouette integration.
-   Negative-space construction.
-   Shape fusion.
-   Typography illustration.
-   Symbolic geometry.
-   Hidden letterforms.

Explain how the elements connect. A concept should have a clear
construction logic.

### Phase 4 --- Design Construction

For the selected direction:

1.  Establish the primary silhouette.
2.  Identify the key letterforms or objects.
3.  Define the relationship between the elements.
4.  Set proportions and visual hierarchy.
5.  Refine positive and negative space.
6.  Choose an appropriate typographic direction.
7.  Balance the composition optically.
8.  Test the design in monochrome.
9.  Check recognition at small sizes.
10. Prepare a clear construction brief.

### Phase 5 --- Image Generation

Create focused image-generation prompts from the approved concept.

The prompt should specify:

-   Exact subjects being fused.
-   How each subject is represented.
-   Which letterforms must remain recognizable.
-   The construction relationship.
-   Composition and silhouette.
-   Line weight and shape language.
-   Desired visual style.
-   Background and output framing.
-   Color restrictions, if any.
-   Explicit exclusions such as mockups, unnecessary text, gradients,
    shadows, and decorative elements.

Do not use vague instructions such as "make a modern animal logo."
Explain precisely how the visual fusion should work.

### Phase 6 --- Refinement

Review generated concepts and identify specific improvements.

Refine:

-   Shape clarity.
-   Letter recognition.
-   Silhouette.
-   Spacing.
-   Optical balance.
-   Proportions.
-   Negative space.
-   Distinctiveness.
-   Small-size performance.

Do not change the concept unnecessarily when only execution needs
improvement.

### Phase 7 --- Finalization

Prepare the selected logo as a coherent identity asset:

-   Primary logo.
-   Standalone symbol.
-   Wordmark.
-   Monochrome version.
-   Positive and reversed versions.
-   Color palette.
-   Typography recommendations.
-   Construction explanation.
-   Usage guidance.
-   Vector-ready artwork or a clearly documented vector reconstruction
    plan.

## 6. Concept Engine

The Concept Engine is a core feature of FORMORA. It separates creative
reasoning from image generation.

Instead of immediately producing images, it should first propose and
explain five distinct concepts.

### Example brief

**Brand:** WOLF\
**Category:** Outdoor equipment\
**Desired association:** Strength, exploration, resilience

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Direction               Construction            Intended meaning
  ----------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------
  01 --- Letter Animal    The `W` becomes a wolf  Brand initial and
                          silhouette              animal identity merge

  02 --- Hidden Symbol    Wolf eyes are           Recognition and
                          integrated into the `O` intelligence

  03 --- Typography       Wordmark strokes        The name becomes the
                          reference wolf anatomy  illustration

  04 --- Negative Space   A wolf profile appears  Discovery and visual
                          between letterforms     intelligence

  05 --- Geometric Fusion The `W` and wolf head   Strength and precision
                          share one geometric     
                          structure               
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

This is an illustrative exercise, not a prescribed design for every
brand.

### Concept selection

After presenting the directions:

1.  Ask the user to select one direction or combine compatible ideas.
2.  Record the selected concept and its rationale.
3.  Develop a construction plan.
4.  Generate a detailed visual prompt.
5.  Produce and review visual explorations.
6.  Refine the strongest result.

The user should be able to request additional directions if the first
five are not suitable.

## 7. Creative and Quality Standards

The following rules should be treated as design constraints:

-   One strong idea is better than several unrelated elements.
-   The symbol must communicate a meaningful relationship.
-   Do not place a generic icon beside a word and call it conceptual
    fusion.
-   Avoid stock-logo aesthetics.
-   Avoid unnecessary gradients, shadows, bevels, and 3D effects.
-   Prefer strong silhouettes and intentional geometry.
-   Use negative space as an active design component.
-   Treat typography as a design material.
-   Preserve the readability of the brand name when required.
-   Do not sacrifice clarity for complexity.
-   Test the design in black and white before applying color.
-   Consider Arabic and English typography independently; do not assume
    that a Latin construction translates directly to Arabic.
-   Never describe a raster image as production-ready vector artwork.
-   Never claim that an image-generation result is a mathematically
    precise or editable vector unless it has actually been reconstructed
    and validated as one.

### Rejection criteria

Reject or revise a concept when:

-   The two visual elements are merely placed next to each other.
-   The hidden meaning is impossible to recognize.
-   The brand name becomes unreadable without a deliberate reason.
-   The silhouette collapses at small sizes.
-   The mark relies on color or effects to communicate its meaning.
-   The concept is generic and could represent almost any business.
-   The visual construction has no clear relationship to the brand.

## 8. Image Generation Methodology

FORMORA should support the image-generation capabilities available in
the active environment. It must not assume that a particular provider,
API, or tool is installed.

### Prompt construction template

Use this structure as a starting point:

``` text
Create a professional conceptual logo for [BRAND NAME], a brand in [INDUSTRY].

CORE CONCEPT:
Combine [PRIMARY ELEMENT] and [SECONDARY ELEMENT] into one unified visual mark.

VISUAL FUSION:
[Explain exactly how the elements connect, overlap, transform, or share geometry.]

TYPOGRAPHY:
[Describe the letterforms, wordmark, readability, and typographic integration.]

CONSTRUCTION:
[Describe silhouette, proportions, negative space, geometry, and composition.]

STYLE:
[Describe the intended visual language.]

COLOR:
[Specify monochrome exploration or the selected palette.]

BACKGROUND:
Clean, plain background.

RESTRICTIONS:
No generic icon placed beside text.
No unnecessary decorative elements.
No mockup presentation.
No random symbols.
No unnecessary gradients, shadows, or 3D effects.
Prioritize conceptual clarity and a recognizable silhouette.

OUTPUT:
A clean, centered logo concept suitable for professional brand identity development.
```

### Generation sequence

1.  Generate monochrome concept explorations.
2.  Compare the visual logic of the outputs.
3.  Select the strongest construction.
4.  Refine the shape and typography.
5.  Explore color only after the concept works in monochrome.
6.  Reconstruct or trace the approved mark as vector artwork when
    required.

Image generation is an exploration and visualization stage. Final
production artwork may require manual vector construction.

## 9. Vector and Production Deliverables

The final output should distinguish between generated imagery and
production artwork.

### Expected logo package

  Deliverable           Description
  --------------------- ----------------------------------------------------
  Primary logo          Main approved brand mark
  Standalone symbol     Icon without the wordmark
  Wordmark              Brand name in its approved typography
  Monochrome            Single-color version
  Reversed              Light mark for dark backgrounds
  Color specification   HEX, RGB, and CMYK values where applicable
  Typography            Typeface and weight recommendations
  Construction notes    Explanation of the concept and geometry
  Usage guidance        Clear space, minimum size, and background guidance
  SVG                   Editable vector version when properly constructed
  PNG                   Raster export for practical use

### SVG quality requirements

When producing SVG:

-   Use clean, valid SVG markup.
-   Use paths for custom shapes.
-   Avoid unnecessary embedded raster images.
-   Keep the viewBox proportional to the design.
-   Ensure fills and strokes are intentional.
-   Check that the SVG renders consistently.
-   Test the result at multiple sizes.
-   Preserve the source file so the artwork can be revised.

If the image-generation tool only produces raster output, explain that a
vector reconstruction step is still required.

The skill should work interactively but should not force unnecessary
questions when the brief already contains sufficient information.

### Activation behavior

When activated:

1.  Inspect the user's request and any supplied reference material.
2.  Identify missing essential brand information.
3.  Ask concise questions only when necessary.
4.  Develop five conceptual directions.
5.  Present the design logic behind each direction.
6.  Let the user select or combine directions.
7.  Build a detailed construction brief.
8.  Generate visual prompts or images using available tools.
9.  Refine the selected result.
10. Prepare final specifications and deliverables.

### Supported input types

The user may provide:

-   Brand name.
-   Business category.
-   Animal or object.
-   Existing logo.
-   Reference image.
-   Reference video or screenshots.
-   A visual idea.
-   Two or more unrelated concepts.
-   A preferred design style.
-   An existing visual identity.

### Example natural-language request

> Create a logo for a coffee brand using an eagle and the letter E. I
> want the eagle to be constructed from the letter itself, not placed

-   Request more concepts.

-   Change the visual style.
-   Preserve the selected concept while refining execution.

## 16. Concept Evaluation Framework

FORMORA should evaluate each concept consistently.

Use a 100-point scoring model:

  Criterion                         Weight
  ------------------------------ ---------
  Conceptual intelligence               20
  Visual integration                    20
  Brand relevance                       15
  Clarity and recognition               15
  Memorability                          10
  Typography quality                    10
  Scalability and reproduction          10
  **Total**                        **100**

### Evaluation guidance

-   **90--100:** Strong candidate for refinement and production.
-   **80--89:** Promising concept; refine execution.
-   **70--79:** Viable direction with meaningful weaknesses.
-   **Below 70:** Reconsider or substantially redesign.

The score is a decision aid, not an objective measurement of artistic
quality. Include a short explanation for each score and identify the
most important improvement.

### Mandatory practical checks

Before approval:

-   Does the design communicate the intended idea?
-   Are the elements genuinely integrated?
-   Can the symbol be recognized without color?
-   Does the design remain clear at small sizes?
-   Is the wordmark readable where required?
-   Does the logo work on light and dark backgrounds?
-   Can the approved artwork be reproduced as vector paths?
-   Does the design have a distinctive relationship to the brand?

## 17. Implementation Notes and Limitations

### Image generation versus vector construction

Image generation can be excellent for visual exploration, but it does
not guarantee:

-   Exact letterform geometry.
-   Correct spelling.
-   Precise negative-space construction.
-   Consistent stroke widths.
-   Editable vector paths.
-   Production-ready SVG output.

Therefore, FORMORA must separate:

1.  **Concept development** --- deciding what the logo should
    communicate.
2.  **Visual exploration** --- generating and comparing visual
    directions.
3.  **Artwork production** --- constructing and validating the final
    vector mark.

### Reference material

The Facebook links in this document are inspiration references, not
verified technical specifications. When the videos are accessible, their
actual design processes should be documented in the reference files.

### Tool availability

The skill should inspect the active environment and use only tools that
are actually available. It must not claim to have generated an image,
created an SVG, or exported a file unless that operation has succeeded.

## Final Product Definition

**FORMORA --- Conceptual Identity Designer**

FORMORA transforms brand ideas into visual identities through
intelligent relationships between typography, letters, animals, objects,
geometry, and negative space.

Its defining workflow is:

**Understand the meaning → Discover visual relationships → Develop
concepts → Fuse visual elements → Construct the mark → Refine →
Deliver.**

The objective is not simply to create a beautiful logo. It is to create
a logo with an idea worth remembering.
